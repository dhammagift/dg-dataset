## 1. What does dukkha mean? Do the suttas define it?

The suttas give both a general characterization and a specific definition. In SN 56.11, the First Noble Truth lists birth, ageing, illness, death, separation from the loved, and not getting what one wants as dukkha, concluding: “In brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha.” SN 22.59 further explains that what is impermanent is dukkha, and that what is dukkha is not fit to be regarded as “mine,” “I,” or “my self.”

Thus dukkha includes suffering, pain, distress, and the unsatisfactoriness or unreliability of conditioned experience. Translating it exclusively as “suffering” can obscure its wider application. The suttas define it contextually, rather than through a single exhaustive lexical definition.

## 2. Is “craving” or “thirst” accurate for taṇhā?

Both are defensible, but neither should be treated as a complete definition. Taṇhā literally evokes thirst; “craving” conveys its psychological force. In SN 56.11, the origin of dukkha is defined as taṇhā: craving for sensual pleasures, craving for existence, and craving for non-existence (kāma-taṇhā, bhava-taṇhā, vibhava-taṇhā).

SN 12.2 places taṇhā after feeling (vedanā) and before clinging (upādāna) in dependent origination. SN 22.99 describes beings as bound by desire and lust for the five aggregates.

“Thirst” preserves the metaphor; “craving” is usually more natural English. Neither means merely wanting something: the relevant issue is the thirst that sustains suffering and bondage.

## 3. Are the two antā in SN 56.11 “extremes”? What does anupagamma imply?

Yes. Antā means ends, bounds, or extremes. SN 56.11 warns against pursuing sensual indulgence (kāmasukhallikānuyoga) and self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga). Calling these “two extremes” is a reasonable translation, though “two ends” preserves the literal sense.

Anupagamma means not approaching, resorting to, or adopting. The Buddha describes the Tathāgata as having awakened to the Middle Way (majjhimā paṭipadā) without resorting to either end.

This wording matters: the Middle Way is not necessarily a compromise or arithmetic midpoint between pleasure and pain. It is a distinct path, identified in the same discourse with the Noble Eightfold Path. The emphasis is on not adopting either of those two practices, rather than on avoiding every position that could be called an extreme.

## 4. Is “impermanent” accurate for anicca, considering SN 22.59?

“Impermanent” is a well-established and defensible translation of anicca, meaning not permanent or not enduring. However, SN 22.59 makes a stronger point than mere transience.

The Buddha asks whether what is anicca is dukkha or sukha. The monks answer categorically: dukkha. This does not mean that every transient experience feels painful. Rather, the discourse treats the five aggregates as unreliable, incapable of providing lasting security, and therefore dukkha in the relevant sense.

The argument continues: what is anicca, dukkha, and subject to change is not fit to be regarded as “mine,” “I,” or “my self.”

Thus “impermanent” is lexically sound, but interpreting anicca as merely “things eventually disappear” misses its role in this argument. Its significance concerns the unsatisfactory nature of what cannot be securely possessed or controlled.

## 5. What is the difference between taṇhā and upādāna, and which comes first?

Taṇhā is thirst or craving; upādāna is clinging, grasping, or taking up. The distinction is between craving for something and the more firmly established appropriation or attachment through which one holds to it.

In the standard dependent-origination sequence, taṇhā comes first, followed by upādāna: “With craving as condition, clinging; with clinging as condition, becoming” (SN 12.2).

The relationship is not merely a distinction between wanting and physically possessing an object. Upādāna includes clinging to sensual pleasures, views, rules and observances, and doctrines of self (SN 12.2).

The sequence describes conditional relations, not necessarily two temporally separated events that can always be identified in everyday experience. Craving conditions clinging, which further sustains the cycle of suffering.

## 6. What does sukha mean? Does it mean happiness, and do the suttas define it?

Sukha can mean happiness, pleasure, ease, comfort, or well-being. Its precise meaning depends on context. The suttas distinguish sensual pleasure from the happiness of renunciation, concentration, and liberation; sukha is not restricted to worldly happiness.

The Buddha explicitly characterizes sukha in SN 36.15, the Nirāmisa Sutta, by distinguishing carnal pleasure (sāmisa sukha) from non-carnal pleasure (nirāmisa sukha) and from a still more refined form of non-carnal pleasure. MN 59 also distinguishes different kinds of pleasure and argues that one pleasure can be abandoned for a superior one.

Therefore, “happiness” is often appropriate, but “pleasure” or “ease” may fit particular passages better. The suttas define and classify sukha contextually; they do not reduce it to one uniform psychological state.
