## 1. What does dukkha mean? Do the suttas define it?

Yes. The suttas give both a broad characterization and specific instances, but no single English equivalent captures every use.

In SN 56.11, the first noble truth enumerates birth, aging, illness, death, association with the disliked, separation from the liked, and not getting what one wants, concluding: saṅkhittena pañcupādānakkhandhā dukkhā — “in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha.”

SN 36.11 distinguishes painful, pleasant, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feelings, so dukkha cannot simply mean physical pain. SN 38.14 identifies the five clinging-aggregates as dukkha.

Thus, suffering, stress, and unsatisfactoriness each capture aspects of its usage. The context determines the emphasis.

## 2. Is “craving” or “thirst” accurate for taṇhā?

Both are defensible, but neither exhausts the suttas’ usage.

Taṇhā literally evokes thirst. In SN 56.11, the second noble truth identifies it as the origin of dukkha: yāyaṃ taṇhā ponobbhavikā nandirāgasahagatā — craving associated with delight and lust, leading to renewed existence. It is distinguished into craving for sensual pleasure (kāma-taṇhā), existence (bhava-taṇhā), and non-existence (vibhava-taṇhā).

SN 12.2 places taṇhā after feeling (vedanā) in dependent origination.

“Craving” conveys compulsive wanting; “thirst” preserves the metaphor. Neither implies that every ordinary preference is necessarily taṇhā. The suttas characterize it by its role in suffering and continued becoming.

## 3. Are the two antā in SN 56.11 “extremes”? What does anupagamma imply?

Yes. Antā means ends, bounds, or extremes. SN 56.11 identifies indulgence in sensual pleasure and devotion to self-mortification as two extremes a renunciant should avoid.

The expression is ete te, bhikkhave, ubho ante anupagamma — “without approaching these two extremes.” Anupagamma means not approaching, resorting to, or adopting them.

This does not necessarily mean the Middle Way is a compromise or midpoint between two positions. The Buddha instead presents a distinct path: the Noble Eightfold Path.

The wording concerns avoiding these two approaches, not merely finding a moderate quantity of pleasure and pain. See SN 56.11.

## 4. Is “impermanent” accurate for anicca, especially in SN 22.59?

“Impermanent” is a well-established translation of anicca, but its implications require care.

In SN 22.59 (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta), the Buddha asks whether what is anicca is dukkha or sukha. The monks answer dukkhaṃ, bhante — “dukkha, venerable sir.” The same discourse explains that what is impermanent, subject to change, and unreliable is unsuitable to regard as “mine,” “I,” or “my self.”

This does not mean that every impermanent event must feel unpleasant. The argument concerns conditioned aggregates as objects of appropriation and identification, not the claim that every passing pleasure is experientially painful.

“Impermanent” captures non-permanence; “inconstant” or “unstable” can sometimes better convey the practical significance.

## 5. What is the difference between taṇhā and upādāna, and which comes first?

Taṇhā is craving or thirst; upādāna is taking up, grasping, or clinging. They are related but distinct links in dependent origination.

SN 12.2 gives the sequence:

Feeling → craving → clinging → becoming (vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna → bhava).

Craving is the thirst that seeks satisfaction or continued existence; clinging is the further appropriation or holding on. SN 12.52 describes how delighting in and welcoming feeling leads to craving, illustrating how one conditions the next.

This is the standard causal sequence, not necessarily a claim that every instance of craving must be followed immediately by an identifiable act of clinging. The distinction matters because the cessation of craving conditions the cessation of clinging and the subsequent links.

## 6. What does sukha mean? Does the suttas define it?

Sukha can mean pleasure, happiness, ease, or well-being. Its meaning depends on context; it is not restricted to either sensory pleasure or an abstract state of happiness.

The suttas distinguish pleasant feeling (sukha-vedanā) from painful and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling (SN 36.11). Elsewhere, they describe the happiness of renunciation, seclusion, and liberation, which is not equivalent to sensual gratification. See MN 139 and MN 36.

MN 59 discusses different kinds of pleasure and happiness, comparing sensual pleasures with those arising from renunciation and meditative attainments.

So the suttas do characterize sukha through its varieties and conditions rather than offering one exhaustive lexical definition. “Pleasure” suits some contexts; “happiness,” “ease,” or “well-being” suits others.
