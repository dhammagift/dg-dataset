## 1. What does dukkha mean? Do the suttas define it?

The suttas offer several characterizations, not one exhaustive lexical definition. In SN 56.11, the first noble truth lists birth, aging, death, sorrow, pain, and association with the disliked, separation from the liked, and not getting what one wants, concluding: “in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha.” In SN 36.11, dukkha includes painful feeling, but the discourse distinguishes painful, pleasant, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feelings. Thus dukkha cannot simply mean physical pain or unpleasant feeling. It encompasses suffering, distress, and the problematic nature of conditioned existence, especially the aggregates subject to clinging. No single English equivalent captures every contextual use.

## 2. Is “craving” or “thirst” an accurate translation of taṇhā?

Both are defensible. Taṇhā literally evokes thirst; “craving” conveys its psychological force. In SN 56.11, the origin of dukkha is identified as taṇhā, which leads to renewed existence and is accompanied by delight and lust. Three forms are specified: craving for sensual pleasures (kāma-taṇhā), existence (bhava-taṇhā), and non-existence (vibhava-taṇhā). SN 12.2 places taṇhā after feeling and before clinging. “Thirst” preserves the metaphor; “craving” reads more naturally in English. Neither should be understood as every desire or intention: the suttas distinguish craving from other forms of wanting, including wholesome aspiration.

## 3. Are the two antā in SN 56.11 “extremes”? What does anupagamma imply?

Yes. Antā literally means ends or boundaries; “extremes” is appropriate in context. SN 56.11 names indulgence in sensual pleasure and devotion to self-mortification as two antā that one gone forth should not pursue. Anupagamma means not approaching, adopting, or resorting to them. It does not necessarily mean avoiding every experience of pleasure or every form of bodily discipline. The middle way is then identified positively as the Noble Eightfold Path. Thus the passage presents more than a compromise between two extremes: it identifies a distinct path of practice leading to knowledge, awakening, and Nibbāna. The precise implication depends on the surrounding description, not the prefix alone.

## 4. Is “impermanent” an accurate translation of anicca?

“Impermanent” is a well-established and generally accurate rendering, but SN 22.59 raises an important interpretive question. The Buddha asks whether what is anicca is dukkha or sukha; the monks answer dukkha. He then reasons that what is impermanent, subject to change, and therefore unreliable cannot rightly be regarded as “mine,” “I,” or “my self.” The argument links impermanence with vulnerability and unsatisfactoriness, rather than equating the two words lexically. Anicca means not permanent, not lasting; dukkha expresses the unsatisfactory or suffering-related character of what is subject to change. The categorical answer concerns the aggregates under examination, not a dictionary definition that makes anicca and dukkha synonyms.

## 5. What is the difference between taṇhā and upādāna, and which comes first?

Taṇhā is craving or thirst; upādāna is clinging, grasping, or appropriation. Craving reaches toward or longs for something; clinging involves taking it up, holding to it, or treating it as a basis of identity and continued existence. In the standard dependent-origination sequence in SN 12.2, feeling conditions craving, craving conditions clinging, and clinging conditions becoming (bhava). Thus taṇhā comes first in that sequence. This is a structural account of conditionality, not necessarily a claim that every instance of craving in ordinary experience must visibly develop into clinging. The terms overlap in practical experience but are not interchangeable in the formula.

## 6. What does sukha mean? Happiness, or something else? Do the suttas define it?

Sukha can mean happiness, pleasure, ease, comfort, or well-being, depending on context. It is not restricted to emotional happiness. In SN 36.11, the Buddha distinguishes pleasant (sukha), painful (dukkha), and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling (adukkhamasukha). In MN 44, bodily and mental pleasure and pain are discussed in relation to feeling. The suttas also describe meditative pleasure and the happiness of liberation, so sukha includes experiences quite different from sensual gratification. The texts characterize it in multiple contexts rather than supplying one exhaustive definition. Translating it as “happiness” can work, but “pleasure” or “ease” may be more accurate for particular passages.

### 2. Taṇhā

“Craving” is a plausible conventional translation, but the Suttas' similes deserve more weight than English convention alone. SN 56.11 describes taṇhā as accompanied by delight and passion; AN 3.76–77 compares it to moisture nourishing the seed. These passages do not straightforwardly define it as wanting. The proposal “clinging” or “adhering” is worth testing, but the evidence cited does not conclusively establish that translation either. SN 12.2 establishes its conditional role, not its English definition.

### 3. Antā and anupagamma

In SN 56.11, antā literally means “ends” or “boundaries”; “extremes” is a defensible contextual translation, though it can suggest excessive degrees. Anupagamma means not approaching or resorting to them. The passage therefore rejects both specified practices and presents the Noble Eightfold Path as the middle way. It does not explicitly define the path as moderation or as a compromise between two permissible ranges.

### 4. Anicca

“Impermanent” remains a defensible lexical translation, but SN 22.59 requires attention to the relationship between impermanence and dukkha. The monks categorically answer that what is anicca is dukkha, not sukha. This supports the teaching that impermanent aggregates are not reliably satisfying; it does not by itself prove that anicca means “unreliable.” Nor does the possibility that pain ends establish that pain is sukha in the passage's sense.

### 5. Taṇhā and upādāna

SN 12.2 explicitly states that taṇhā conditions upādāna, which conditions becoming. That order is not in dispute. “Craving → clinging” is conventional and broadly intelligible, but the fuel similes in SN 44.9 make “sustaining” a meaningful alternative for upādāna. Conversely, the moisture and delight similes for taṇhā do not establish that “clinging” is its uniquely correct translation. The proposed pair remains a hypothesis, not a demonstrated lexical equivalence.

### 6. Sukha

SN 48.36 defines the faculty sukhindriya as bodily pleasant feeling, distinguishing it from mental pleasure, somanassa. This is strong evidence against translating that particular technical term as mental happiness. However, it does not establish that sukha everywhere means bodily comfort: the wider discourses also describe meditative pleasure. Context matters, and “pleasant feeling,” “comfort,” or “happiness” may fit different occurrences.
