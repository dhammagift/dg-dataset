## Step 1 (cold)

1. Dukkha
In the suttas, dukkha is broader than “pain” and narrower than a vague “suffering.” The clearest definition is the First Noble Truth formula: “birth is dukkha, aging is dukkha, illness is dukkha, death is dukkha ... association with the unloved ... separation from the loved ... not getting what one wants ... in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha” (SN 56.11; also DN 22, MN 141). The suttas define it chiefly by enumeration and by identifying the five upādānakkhandhas as dukkha.
2. Taṇhā: craving or thirst?
Both are defensible. Taṇhā literally means “thirst.” The suttas use it as the wanting that “seeks delight here and there”: kāma-taṇhā, bhava-taṇhā, vibhava-taṇhā (SN 56.11). Since English “thirst” naturally conveys a compelling urge, it preserves the metaphor. “Craving” better conveys the psychological function. Judged by usage, “craving” is usually the better explanatory translation; “thirst” is the more literal one.
3. Antā and anupagamma in SN 56.11
Antā literally means “ends,” “extremes,” or “terminal positions.” “Extremes” is standard and reasonable. The Buddha says a renunciant should not anupagamma them, literally “going toward,” “approaching,” or “resorting to” them. The middle way is therefore not necessarily a mathematical midpoint between two quantities, but a path that avoids falling into either mode of practice: sensual indulgence and self-mortification.
4. Anicca and “impermanent”
“Impermanent” is common but not always ideal. Anicca means “not lasting,” “unstable,” “inconstant.” In SN 22.59 the sequence is: “Is form permanent or anicca?” “Anicca.” “What is anicca, is it dukkha or sukha?” “Dukkha.” The argument works because what is unstable and unreliable cannot be taken as satisfactory. “Impermanent” captures part of this, but may understate the sense of ongoing instability and unreliability present in the text.
5. Taṇhā and upādāna
Taṇhā is craving/desire; upādāna is clinging, grasping, appropriation. In dependent origination, taṇhā conditions upādāna: “with craving as condition, clinging” (taṇhāpaccayā upādānaṃ) (SN 12.2, SN 12.1). Thus craving comes first. One may crave an object without yet fully identifying with or appropriating it; upādāna is the stronger act of taking hold.
6. Sukha
Sukha means pleasantness, ease, comfort, well-being, happiness, depending on context. The suttas contrast it with dukkha in feeling (vedanā): pleasant, painful, neither-painful-nor-pleasant (e.g., MN 59). They also speak of meditative and renunciant sukha (MN 36, MN 139). The suttas do not give a single dictionary-style definition; they define it functionally through experience and contrast. “Happiness” is often correct, but sometimes “pleasure,” “ease,” or “well-being” fits better.

(b) How should MN 141:10.2 be read?
The difficulty is real.
The sentence contains:
soka-parideva-dukkha-domanass-upāyāsāpi dukkhā
and MN 141 subsequently defines:

* dukkha = bodily pain (16)
* domanassa = mental pain (17)

If the predicate dukkhā is translated as a broader "suffering," then one item in the list (dukkha) becomes bodily pain while the predicate changes meaning. That is possible linguistically, but the text itself does not explicitly announce such a shift.
Read with the supplied definitions applied consistently, the sentence means roughly:
sorrow, lamentation, bodily pain, mental pain, and distress are painful.
That reading is admittedly tautological regarding the dukkha member of the list ("pain is painful"), but it preserves the definitions actually given in the discourse.
So on textual grounds alone, I do not think my earlier reading was as well supported as the document's criticism suggests.
